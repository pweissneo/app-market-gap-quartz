Monetisation - Consumable Add-Ons and Battle Pass

Consumable add-ons and battle pass subscriptions turn one-time game players into recurring revenue streams. Players spend small amounts repeatedly for temporary benefits (extra lives, power-ups, season content), or commit to a season-track that rewards ongoing engagement. The model generates more per-user revenue than any other IAP pattern in mobile gaming — and is starting to appear in fitness and productivity apps.

Pattern Definition

Consumable Add-Ons: In-app purchases that are consumed upon use and must be repurchased — extra lives, game currency bundles, power-ups, level packs, loot boxes, stamina/energy refills. Each purchase is discrete; revenue recurs only if the developer makes repurchase compelling.

Battle Pass (Season Pass): A time-bound progression track (typically 8–12 weeks) that rewards players for engagement. A free track and a paid “premium” track. The paid track typically costs $10–15/season and offers exclusive cosmetic or functional rewards. The key mechanic: progress resets each season, creating repeat purchase pressure.

Key psychological driver: Loss aversion + sunk cost + FOMO. Players who buy the pass and fall behind are motivated to catch up; if they don’t use it, they feel they’ve wasted the purchase and buy again next season.

Market Scale

Mobile gaming dominated consumer spending in apps in 2025, reaching $82.7B in total consumer spend (2.2% YoY growth), with games accounting for over half of all iOS app spending and over a third of Google Play spending. Strategy games generate the most revenue by genre. China, US, and Japan account for over 50% of mobile game spending.

  • Engagement window: 8–12 weeks per season. Players who engage daily generate far more ad revenue and are far more likely to convert.
  • Seasonal reset: All progress clears; players must re-earn rewards. This is a feature, not a bug — it creates repeat purchase cycles.
  • FOMO mechanics: Missed rewards from prior seasons are gone permanently. This drives purchase decisions mid-season (“I don’t want to miss this again”).
  • Free track bait: The free track provides enough reward to demonstrate value; the gap to premium creates urgency.

Consumable Add-Ons: Sub-Patterns

1. Soft Currency (Gold/Gems/Coins)

  • Earned slowly for free; purchased for real money
  • Used to buy upgrades, cosmetics, or special events
  • Creates a psychological exchange rate: “$1 = 100 gems = 1 rare item”
  • Examples: Candy Crush Saga, Genshin Impact, Royal Revolt

2. Hard Currency (Diamonds/Gems — Premium)

  • Purchased directly with real money; sometimes earned in small amounts
  • Used for the most valuable items — creates “whale” spending paths
  • Common in RPGs and gacha games
  • Risk: regulatory scrutiny if odds are not disclosed

3. Loot Boxes / Gacha

  • Randomized rewards; player doesn’t know what they’ll get
  • Highest ARPU but faces gambling regulation in Belgium, Netherlands, China
  • Apple/Google now require disclosure of odds in their stores
  • Trend: shifting toward “pity system” (guaranteed rare item after N pulls)

4. Energy / Stamina Systems

  • Gameplay requires energy that refills over time or via IAP
  • Creates natural purchase pressure at session’s end
  • Most aggressive monetization mechanic; can drive uninstalls if too punishing
  • Examples: Candy Crush, Cookie Jam, most match-3 games

5. Expansions / Level Packs

  • One-time purchasable content that adds permanent value
  • Less aggressive than consumables; players know exactly what they’re getting
  • Works well in puzzle and adventure games
  • Examples: Monument Valley, several Square Enix mobile titles

6. Battle Pass / Season Pass (see above)

  • Time-bound premium track tied to a “season”
  • Combines subscription psychology with limited-time urgency
  • Highest perceived value per dollar of any IAP format
  • Examples: Fortnite, PUBG Mobile, Clash Royale, Brawl Stars

Gap Implications

For Gaming App Builders

  1. Battle pass is mandatory for any competitive/multiplayer game: Players expect it. A game without a seasonal content cycle loses engagement to games with one. The pass funds ongoing development costs.

  2. Consumable energy systems are double-edged: They drive short-term revenue but also drive uninstalls when players feel punished. The sweet spot is “soft energy” — slow natural regen with optional IAP — rather than hard limits.

  3. Season content cycle must match engagement data: If the season is too long and players disengage, revenue collapses. If too short, the FOMO effect weakens. 8–10 weeks is the industry sweet spot for mid-core games.

  4. iOS vs Android battle pass pricing: iOS users generate 2.5x more revenue per user. Battle pass pricing on Android should be 50–70% of iOS pricing to maximize conversion while preserving revenue.

For Non-Gaming App Builders

  1. Seasonal content packs are the battle-pass equivalent: Bundle premium features/content into a time-bounded “season” (30-day, 90-day) with a clear start/end. Renewal is not automatic — users must re-purchase, which creates engagement pressure.

  2. Consumable add-ons in habit apps: Streak repair, “freeze” days, extra habit slots, one-time achievement unlocks — consumable IAP for habit apps mirror the energy system but with lower friction. The key difference: habit app consumables should reduce guilt rather than create frustration.

  3. The battle pass works best when social proof exists: Leaderboards, shared progress, friends who can see achievements. Without a social layer, the FOMO mechanic of battle passes is muted.
