Monetisation — Paid-Up-Front One-Time Purchase

A paid-up-front model charges a single fixed fee for lifetime access to an app, with no recurring billing. The user pays once and owns the license permanently (or for a major version).

How It Works

  • User pays a one-time price at download/install
  • No subscription, no recurring charge
  • Updates within the same major version are typically free
  • Platform take-rate applies (Apple ~30%, Google ~30%) on the full price
  • Some apps offer tiered one-time tiers (Standard / Pro) without subscription
  • iOS and Android both support this natively; no special API required

Market Share and Benchmarks

Paid apps (non-subscription) make up less than 5% of both the Apple App Store and Google Play Store by download volume, and contribute an even smaller share of total revenue. The vast majority of app store revenue comes from subscriptions and in-app purchases.

The majority of paid apps that do exist on app stores are priced **under 1∗∗.Appspricedabove20 are rare and almost exclusively in professional or creative tool categories.

Average app pricing benchmarks (US App Store, 2026):

  • Average weekly subscription: $11.88
  • Average monthly subscription: $17.53
  • Average annual subscription: $103.76

By contrast, successful one-time purchase apps in the Mac/iOS ecosystem typically price between 20–50 for full-feature perpetual licenses.

79% of all app spending across both platforms flows through iOS subscriptions specifically, indicating that the one-time purchase model is heavily concentrated in the small non-subscription segment.

Where One-Time Purchase Works

Creative and Productivity Tools

The most viable category for one-time purchase on mobile mirrors the Mac software pattern: professional-grade creative and productivity tools where the user has a clear job-to-be-done and values certainty over ongoing features.

Examples (mobile or cross-platform):

  • Things 3 by Cultured Code — task management, $49.99 one-time (iOS/macOS, each platform sold separately). Remains one-time despite entire task-manager category moving to subscription. Cited as the “gold-standard counterexample.”
  • Pixelmator Pro — photo editing, $49.99 one-time on iOS App Store. Kept one-time pricing even after Apple acquired the team in late 2024. ML-based upscaling, raw editing, vector tools.
  • Ulysses — writing app, $19.99 one-time or subscription. Demonstrates both models can coexist for the same product.
  • Tot by Iconfactory — minimal menu-bar notepad, $20 one-time. Seven colored dots, deliberately scoped, no subscription.
  • Acorn by Flying Meat — Photoshop alternative, $39.99 one-time (Mac). Demonstrates sustained one-time viability for niche creative tools.

Niche Professional Tools

Apps targeting specific professional workflows (medical coding, legal reference, aviation navigation) can command 50–200+ one-time fees because the professional buyer is reimbursed by an employer or deducts the cost, and because the tool is mission-critical.

Games (Ported Titles)

Console and PC game ports to mobile (e.g., classic titles, strategy games) still sell as paid apps. This is one of the few mobile genres where paid-up-front is common.

Where One-Time Purchase Fails

Consumer Utility Apps

Apps competing in the commodity productivity, health, or lifestyle space cannot sustain one-time pricing because:

  1. Consumer willingness to pay is too low to recoup development cost
  2. Free competitors with ad revenue dominate the category
  3. Platform take-rates on a $1–3 one-time sale leave almost nothing after Apple/Google’s 30%

Apps Requiring Ongoing Server Costs

Any app that runs server-side infrastructure (cloud sync, AI processing, backend databases) has a structural problem with one-time pricing: the marginal cost per user never goes to zero, so a large enough user base creates an unsustainable cost center.

Categories Dominated by Subscription Incumbents

If the market leader is subscription-based and has network effects (e.g., note-taking with shared workspaces), a one-time-purchase entrant faces both a pricing disadvantage (they must undercut the subscription on annual cost while having no recurring revenue) and a feature-gap against an entrenched player with continuous development funding.

Platform-Specific Considerations

iOS

  • Apple allows paid apps with no IAP (pure one-time), or paid with optional IAP
  • Apps can offer “lifetime” subscription tiers (typically 2–3× annual cost) which function as one-time purchases from a user perspective
  • App Store review guidelines permit one-time pricing without requiring justification
  • 30% platform fee applies; reduced to 15% for apps with under $1M revenue

Android

  • Google Play similarly supports paid apps with one-time purchase pricing
  • Regional pricing variations allow lower price points in emerging markets
  • ABP (Alternate Billing System) requirements in EU/South Korea allow developers to use third-party payment processors for a 26% commission (lower than 30%), improving economics for one-time purchase apps in those markets

Cross-Platform Differences

  • The Android ecosystem has slightly more tolerance for paid apps in utility categories than iOS, likely due to the broader device range and historically lower average consumer spending
  • Both platforms show declining paid-app share year-over-year as more developers convert to subscription

Economics for Developers

Revenue per user (one-time):

  • 10price×707.00/user
  • No further revenue from that user unless they buy upgrades

Revenue per user (subscription, 2-year horizon):

  • 10/month×24months×70168/user

The subscription model generates 24× more revenue per user over two years. This is why most developers who can convert to subscription do.

When one-time purchase makes economic sense:

  • Development cost is low enough that acquisition volume compensates for lower per-user revenue
  • App has strong word-of-mouth / low paid acquisition cost
  • Target user is a professional who pays once and uses heavily (high retention)
  • No server costs (or server costs are negligible)
  • Competitors are all subscription, making one-time a differentiator

Competitive Dynamics

The one-time purchase model is increasingly a positioning statement rather than a mainstream strategy. Successful one-time-purchase apps differentiate on values (privacy, no recurring billing, ownership) and accept lower total revenue in exchange for loyal user bases who resent subscriptions.

Perceived advantages to consumers:

  • No credit card ever expires
  • No subscription reminder emails
  • Feeling of “ownership”
  • Math: anything used more than 12–18 months costs less than an equivalent subscription

Perceived disadvantages to consumers:

  • No continuous updates or new features without additional purchase
  • Risk of app abandonment if developer exits
  • No cross-device sync unless included (often requires separate purchase on iOS per platform)

Key Takeaway

The paid-up-front model is viable only in specific niches — professional creative tools, niche utilities with no server costs, and ported games — where the value is clear, the development cost is recoverable through volume, and the competitive set has already moved to subscription (creating an opening). For most consumer categories, the model is structurally outcompeted by subscription and cannot generate equivalent LTV. The model works best as a premium positioning signal for indie developers in categories where subscription fatigue is real and user trust is a differentiator.
