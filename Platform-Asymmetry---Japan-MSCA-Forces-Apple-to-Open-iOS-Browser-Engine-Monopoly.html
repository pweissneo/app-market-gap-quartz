Platform Asymmetry — Japan MSCA Forces Apple to Open iOS Browser Engine Monopoly

What Changed

Japan’s Mobile Software Competition Act (MSCA), effective December 18, 2025, prohibits Apple from preventing app providers from using alternative browser engines on iOS. The law directly targets Apple’s long-standing WebKit-only mandate for all browsers on iPhone and iPad.

The MSCA goes further than the EU’s Digital Markets Act (which allowed alternative engines in the EU since iOS 17.4 but produced no major non-WebKit browsers). Japan’s law prohibits Apple from imposing “unreasonable technical restrictions” or “excessive financial burdens” that render alternative engine adoption impractical — even if technically permitted.

Apple responded in iOS 26.2 by introducing two entitlements for Japan:

  • Web Browser Engine Entitlement — for dedicated browser apps (Chrome, Firefox, Edge, etc.)
  • Embedded Browser Engine Entitlement — for apps embedding alternative engines in in-app browsing

Requirements to qualify: ≥90% Web Platform Tests pass rate, ≥80% Test262 pass rate, memory-safe development practices, public vulnerability disclosure policy, modern TLS (1.2/1.3), third-party cookies blocked by default. Entitlements are Japan-only.

Apple must also show a default browser selection screen in Safari on iPhone in Japan.

Why This Creates a Gap

Before December 2025, iOS users worldwide were locked into WebKit rendering. Chrome, Firefox, Edge, Opera, Brave, and Vivaldi on iOS were all WebKit-based — identical rendering to Safari. No effective browser competition existed on iOS anywhere.

The gap is asymmetric:

DimensionBefore MSCAAfter MSCA (Japan)
Browser enginesWebKit-only globallyNon-WebKit now legal in Japan
Chrome/Firefox on iOSWebKit skinNative Blink/Gecko in Japan
Web app capabilitiesCrippled by WebKit limitationsFull engine APIs available
Market for browser innovationZero — all engines identicalNew competition possible

The EU DMA approach failed: Apple imposed so many technical and procedural restrictions that years after DMA compliance, no major non-WebKit browser exists on EU iOS. Japan’s law was explicitly designed to close this loophole.

Who Could Exploit This

Browser developers (Google, Mozilla, Microsoft, Brave, Opera) can now ship native-engine iOS browsers in Japan — something impossible everywhere else. This creates a first-mover advantage: the first non-WebKit browser to gain traction in Japan could establish brand loyalty before others arrive.

Web app developers can build PWAs in Japan that access APIs previously WebKit-blocked — WebAssembly threading, WebGL 2, advanced Web APIs that required non-WebKit engines.

Privacy/security tool developers can leverage engine-specific features (e.g., Firefox’s Enhanced Tracking Protection runs better on Gecko than on a ported WebKit).

Monetisation Path

Browser monetisation is primarily ad-platform-adjacent (Google default search deals, Mozilla search partnerships). However, the gap is more about market positioning than direct browser revenue:

  • Enterprise browser: A non-WebKit browser targeting Japanese enterprises with superior security dashboard, IT policy management, and compliance reporting could charge subscription fees.
  • Specialised rendering app: Apps that depend on accurate rendering (web development tools, design previews, educational content) could monetise through pro tiers.
  • Web app platform: A hosting platform for web apps that require non-WebKit capabilities (previously impossible on iOS) could charge developers.

Open Questions

  • Will major browser vendors prioritises Japan launches, or will the entitlement requirements (90% WPT, security compliance, memory-safe practices) slow adoption?
  • Could Apple expand iOS 26.2-style entitlements to other regions, making Japan a testing ground for global browser engine competition?
  • Will Japan’s law influence the UK (also pressuring Apple on browser engines) or other Asian markets?
